About ClimateGuard

Methodology, data sources, architecture and known limitations.

Important: what this is and is not

This is a student research project. The predictions and risk assessments are generated by a machine learning model trained on reanalysis data.

  • Predictions use ERA5 reanalysis grid data, not official IMD station observations. ERA5 grid-cell values may differ from ground-station readings.
  • Risk levels and adaptation recommendations are project-defined operational categories, not official government warning levels.
  • This system is not designed or intended for use in emergency response planning.
  • During heat emergencies, always follow advisories from the India Meteorological Department and local authorities.

Project overview

ClimateGuard predicts heatwave events one day in advance for five major Indian cities. It combines a Random Forest classifier trained on 35 years of ERA5 climate data with a deterministic expert rule engine inspired by IMD heatwave criteria.

The system provides a binary prediction (heatwave/normal), a probability estimate, a risk level, expert rule assessments, and adaptation guidance to help users understand heat risk conditions.

System architecture

1

Data ingestion

ERA5 reanalysis data (ECMWF via Open-Meteo API) covering 1990–2025. Daily variables: maximum, minimum and mean 2m temperature, relative humidity, wind speed and gusts, surface pressure, shortwave radiation, reference evapotranspiration and precipitation.

↓
2

Feature engineering

110 features including lag values (T−1, T−2, T−3, T−7), rolling means and extremes (3-day and 7-day), departure from climatological normals, anomaly z-scores, trend slopes, and calendar and city encodings. All windows shift by at least one day to prevent future-data leakage.

↓
3

ML prediction

Random Forest Classifier (300 trees, max depth 10) with random undersampling to handle class imbalance (roughly 1% of days are heatwave days). Decision threshold fixed at 0.70 based on validation-set F1 optimisation. Chronological train/val/test split avoids data leakage; test period is 2023–2025.

↓
4

Risk engine

Maps probability to four risk levels: LOW (0.00–0.30), MODERATE (0.30–0.60), HIGH (0.60–0.80), EXTREME (0.80–1.00). Generates adaptation guidance tailored to each level. Thresholds are project-defined.

↓
5

Expert rules

Seven deterministic rules derived from IMD-inspired criteria: Extreme Temperature, Persistent Heat, High Nighttime Temperature, Compounded Heat Stress, Vulnerable Population Alert, Outdoor Exposure Warning, and Hydration Reminder. Each rule has a fixed threshold and fires independently of the ML model.

Heatwave definition

This project uses an IMD-inspired operational definition applied to ERA5 grid data. A heatwave day requires at least two consecutive qualifying days in a city-specific threshold band.

Plains cities (Delhi, Lucknow, Nagpur, Ahmedabad): a qualifying day is Tmax ≥ 40°C with departure ≥ 4.5°C from the 1990–2020 climatological normal, or Tmax ≥ 45°C unconditionally.

Coastal cities (Mumbai): a qualifying day is Tmax ≥ 37°C with departure ≥ 4.5°C.

Mumbai has zero positive heatwave examples across the entire 1990–2025 period under this definition, which is consistent with its maritime climate. The model cannot be evaluated for Mumbai heatwave performance.

Cities covered

  • New Delhi — Delhi · Plains · 28.61°N, 77.21°E
  • Lucknow — Uttar Pradesh · Plains · 26.85°N, 80.95°E
  • Nagpur — Maharashtra · Plains · 21.15°N, 79.09°E
  • Ahmedabad — Gujarat · Plains · 23.02°N, 72.57°E
  • Mumbai — Maharashtra · Coastal · 19.08°N, 72.88°E

Known limitations

  1. ERA5 reanalysis, not station data. ERA5 is a gridded atmospheric reanalysis product. Temperature values at a 0.25° grid cell may differ from IMD station readings at the same location.
  2. IMD-inspired labels. The heatwave ground truth was constructed using IMD criteria applied to ERA5 data. It is not an official IMD dataset.
  3. qualifying_day feature. One of the 110 features (qualifying_day) uses the same threshold conditions as the label definition. It is computed from today’s data only (no future leakage), but its strong correlation with the target is a known limitation that is documented in the model contract.
  4. City coverage gaps. Ahmedabad has no positive test-period examples; all 32 heatwave days fall in the training window. Mumbai has no positive examples at all. Neither city can be properly evaluated.
  5. Class imbalance. Heatwave days are about 1% of all days. The model uses random undersampling during training, which may cause it to miss rare edge-case heatwaves.
  6. Model horizon. Trained on 1990–2022 data. Climate patterns may drift past the training distribution.
  7. No drift detection. There is no automated check for distributional shift between historical training data and current inputs.

Technology

Python 3.11 backend: scikit-learn (Random Forest), pandas, NumPy, SHAP (optional), FastAPI, Uvicorn. Frontend: plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript with Chart.js (charts) and Leaflet.js (map). No build step or package manager.

ERA5 data via Open-Meteo Historical API (no API key required).
